# Face to Face (BBC, 6 May 1960) – Interview with **Carl Gustav Jung**

## Document Provenance

This document is a **custom scribe draft** written while repeatedly reviewing the interview to preserve tone, pacing, and meaning.

Quotations are drawn from the interview itself; explanatory sentences are the scribe’s connective narrative. Where exact wording is uncertain, it is presented as a scribed highlight rather than a verbatim transcript.

## 1. Introduction – Who Was Carl Jung?

*Carl Gustav Jung* (1875–1961) was a Swiss psychiatrist and psychotherapist and the founder of **Analytical Psychology**, known for his work on psychological types, complexes, and the collective unconscious (Wikipedia).

The interview referenced in this document is an episode of the BBC’s *Face to Face* recorded at Jung’s home in Küsnacht, Switzerland, and broadcast on 6 May 1960 (BBC Genome).

## 2. The BBC Series *Face to Face* – Supplementary Details

*Face to Face* was a BBC television interview series broadcast between 1959 and 1962. The interviewer, John Freeman, was rarely shown on camera; the visual emphasis remained on the subject, with close-ups and long takes. Episode titles featured caricatures by Feliks Topolski and the theme music used an excerpt from Berlioz’s overture to *Les Francs-juges* (BBC Genome).

The Jung edition was filmed on location at Jung’s home and is notable for its range: childhood and family, education, Freud, religion, death, and the future of humanity (Jungian Analytic Society).

## 3. Scribe Draft

### Introduction

Carl Gustav Jung (Professor Jung), born in 1875, stands alongside Freud as one of the founding fathers of psychology. Still working at 84 (scribed impression), he is one of the most honoured living psychiatrists, and history will recall him as one of the greatest physicians of all time.

### Family

He has lived in the same house by Lake Zurich for approximately fifty years.

Professor Jung has nineteen grandchildren and eight great-grandchildren, with another on the way. They are not afraid of him; in fact, they play with his hats. He acknowledges the subtle, underlying beauty of having such a large living crowd emerge from himself.

### First Memory of Individual Consciousness

Upon being asked about his first memory of interaction with his own consciousness, Dr Jung responds quickly and intuitively: "That was in my eleventh year, on my way to school. I stepped out of a mist. It was as though I had been in a mist—I was walking in a mist—and suddenly I walked out of the mist and knew, 'I am.' I am what I am. And then I thought, 'What had I been before?' And I realised that I had been in a mist, not knowing how to differentiate myself from things. I was just one thing among many things."

He reflects: "As far as I can remember, nothing had happened before this sudden coming to consciousness."

### Early Childhood and Parental Relationships

He shares his early years of childhood and his relationships with his father and mother. Upon being asked whom he was closer to and whether his father punished him, he asserts: "Of course one is always more intimate with the mother, but when it comes to personal feeling, I had a better relationship with my father, who was predictable. To me, my mother was very problematic in some way. In the day she was fine, but during the night she used to become frightening. I was afraid of her during the night."

He acknowledges that fear had never been an element of his relationship with his father and that his father accepted him as he was.

### Belief

When asked about his spiritual stance and whether he believes in God, Professor Jung replies, "It is difficult to answer," and then says, "I know. I don't need to believe. I know."

### Career

The interview moves to his career and what led him to decide to become a doctor. In reply, Carl Jung refers to the career path as merely an "opportunistic" choice—a strategic decision to become a doctor. He explains his options at the time: a career path to become a schoolteacher or headmaster, which he had no intention of pursuing. He reveals the circumstances: "Originally, I wanted to be an archaeologist—Egyptology, or something of that sort. But I hadn't the money; the study was too expensive."

He adds: "My second love belonged to nature, particularly zoology. When I began my studies, I enrolled in the so-called philosophical faculty through natural sciences. But I soon realised that the career before me would make a schoolmaster of me. I never thought I had any chance to get any further because we had no money at all."

Professor Jung explains the mismatch between circumstances and personal goals—it simply did not align with his expectations because he did not want to be a schoolmaster. That is when he thought of his grandfather's occupation as a doctor and realised that the academic curriculum would provide him with a broader scope to implement his ideas and interests, offering a better chance to pursue them into a tangible career path.

He explains: "Teaching was not quite what I was looking for," describing the deeper desires that guided him to ultimately create timeless analytical studies on the human psyche and establish an entirely new field of study and service for people who struggle with managing various aspects of their daily lives and thoughts. He exclaims: "The idea of doing something useful with human beings appealed to me."

### The Stolen Thesis

When asked whether he had any difficulties during his training period, Dr Jung shares a personal story and provides insight into one of the most significant moments of the interview. In explaining an incident from his early years, he captures the nuances of how the human psyche, persona, memory, and emotions all work intricately together—making clear the gap between how much we understand and how much remains to be discovered.

He says: "Particularly, I had difficulty with certain teachers who didn't believe I could write a decent thesis. I remember in one case, the teacher was ready to discuss the papers in class with the pupils..."

Dr Jung recounts an earlier memory of a class he attended in which the teacher doubted his expertise in writing good thesis papers and also, when discussing the evaluated papers in class—starting from the best at the top all the way to the last—failed to mention Professor Jung's paper or name. Only towards the end did the teacher exclaim with sarcasm and accuse Dr Jung of having stolen the thesis.

Reflecting on the memory, it is as if Dr Jung almost time-travels to that moment. He shares the classroom scene while demonstrating by constantly knocking on the wood, almost inviting the viewer to pay attention to the collision of these psychological elements and the shifts the human mind can undergo.

He quotes the teacher: "There is still one paper left over, and that is the one by Jung. That would be by far the best paper if it hadn't been copied." Indicating to Jung: "You are a thief, Jung. And if I knew how you stole it, I would throw you out of school."

"I got mad because that was the one thesis where I had worked the most, because the theme was interesting—in contrast to all the other themes, which were not at all interesting to me," Jung exclaims. He continues: "Now, that was a serious thing to me, because if I was thrown out of school, what then?" This further signifies his feeling about the incident; to him, the whole situation must have seemed paradoxical.

### Discussion about Freud

"You see, I finished my studies in 1900, and I met Freud much later, but I had already studied his dream interpretation and his study of hysteria."

When asked what kind of man Freud was, Jung replies: "Well, he was a complicated man. Once he decided something, that was it."

### On Death

"Yes, it is an end, and we are not quite certain about this end. Because, you know, there are these peculiar faculties of the psyche that are not confined to space and time. You can have dreams or visions of the future. You can see around corners—only ignorance denies these facts. It is quite evident that they do exist and have existed always.

Now, this shows that the psyche, in part at least, is not dependent on these confinements. And then what? The psyche is not under that obligation to live in time and space alone—and obviously, it doesn't. Then, to that extent, the psyche is not limited to those laws. And that means a practical continuation of life—a sort of psychical existence beyond time and space."
